\section{Hotword Conditioning}
\label{sec:hotwords}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{subsec:hotword-setup}

We evaluate hotword behaviour with two evaluation protocols that
differ in how the per-utterance hotword list is constructed.

\paragraph{Oracle prompt (random padding).}
The CommonVoice 17 hotword test splits ship with a per-utterance
ground-truth list of rare entities (mined offline) and a per-utterance
QA acceptance flag. For a target prompt size $k$, the inference
client pads each utterance's list to $k$ entries by mixing the
ground-truth hotwords with random distractors drawn from the same
corpus's global hotword pool. No online hard negatives are added at
evaluation time, in contrast to training
(Section~\ref{subsec:hotword-pool}). The $k=0$ row drops the hotword
prompt line entirely (mirroring the training-time fallback) and is
nearly identical to the corresponding row of
Tables~\ref{tab:asr_zh}--\ref{tab:asr_en}. We report CER for Mandarin
and WER for English. Because the per-utterance ground-truth list is
revealed to the model up to random padding, this protocol gives an
upper bound on the model's prompt-driven hotword behaviour and does
not test the retriever.

\paragraph{Retrieve client (retrieval-based padding).}
The retrieve client replaces ground-truth knowledge with a
per-utterance top-$K$ output of the retrieval operator described in
Section~\ref{subsec:retrieve-algorithm}. The operator takes the
audio and a much larger pool $\mathcal{H}$ (here the union of all
ground-truth lists in the test split) and returns a $K$-element
subset whose content is not guaranteed to contain the ground-truth
term. We report two families of metrics: retrieval-level recall and
biased / unbiased WER on the downstream transcript.

\paragraph{Training exposure.}
Training-time hotword exposure follows
Section~\ref{subsec:hotword-pool}: each sample's prompt is built from
\textbf{real terms} (each ground-truth term independently dropped
with probability $0.05$), \textbf{up to 10 hard negatives} from the
char-overlap + bigram-Jaccard + pinyin-syllable retriever, and the
\textbf{remainder of $n_\text{distractor}\!\in\![1, 20]$ slots} from
the global pool. The hotword prompt line itself is kept with
probability $0.8$. Training and evaluation sweeps therefore cover
the same prompt-length regime; the retrieve client additionally
exercises the discriminative-gap and miss-recall behaviour covered
by the training augmentation in Section~\ref{subsec:hotword-pool}.

\subsection{Retrieve Algorithm}
\label{subsec:retrieve-algorithm}

\paragraph{Problem formalisation.}
Let the global hotword pool be $\mathcal{H} = \{h_1, \dots, h_N\}$
with $N$ up to $10^3$--$10^4$ in deployment. The naive decoder
$\hat{y} = \arg\max P(y \mid x, \mathcal{H})$ is infeasible: it
exceeds the LLM context window, dilutes attention across many
irrelevant candidates, and encourages the LLM to copy hotword
fragments when the audio signal is ambiguous. We replace it by a
retrieval operator $\mathcal{R}\!:\!(x, \mathcal{H}) \to
\mathcal{H}^\prime \subset \mathcal{H}$ with $|\mathcal{H}^\prime|
= K \ll N$ and decode
$\hat{y} = \arg\max P(y \mid x, \mathcal{R}(x, \mathcal{H}))$.
Increasing $K$ raises Recall@$K$ but proportionally increases
attention dilution and hallucination risk, so $K$ is a deliberate
operating-point choice rather than a parameter to maximise.
Recall@$K$ itself is, under the assumption that an un-retrieved
hotword is necessarily missed at the ASR stage, an approximate
upper bound on the downstream hotword-hit rate; the operator must
therefore be evaluated in its own right rather than only through
downstream WER.

\paragraph{Two-stage retrieve-then-bias paradigm.}
We decompose $\mathcal{R}$ into a coarse decoder, a text retriever
and a fine decoder (\Cref{fig:retrieve}, left panel),
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{R}(x, \mathcal{H})
   = r\!\left(g(x),\, \mathcal{H}\right),\qquad
  \hat{y} = f\!\left(x,\, \mathcal{R}(x, \mathcal{H})\right),
  \label{eq:retrieve-decompose}
\end{equation}
where $g$ is a no-hotword forward pass through AmphionASR that
returns a coarse transcript $\tilde{y}$, $r$ is a parameter-free
text-similarity operator that returns the top-$K$ subset of
$\mathcal{H}$ closest to $\tilde{y}$, and $f$ is a second forward
pass through AmphionASR with the retrieved hotword list inserted in
the prompt. The decomposition projects retrieval from audio space
into text space: a hotword $h$ that is actually spoken in $x$
leaves a same-pronunciation or same-character footprint along the
best acoustic path, which $\tilde{y}$ exposes. Audio-space
alternatives such as the speech retriever of
Min et al.~\cite{min2024speechrag} (\Cref{fig:retrieve}, right
panel) require an additional audio-to-text alignment network and
hotword--utterance pair supervision that our training data does
not provide; CTC-assisted
contextual ASR~\cite{yang2024ctc} uses a CTC auxiliary head as $g$
to provide $\tilde{y}$. Our $g$ is the no-hotword pass of the same
LLM-based decoder used for $f$, which keeps the upstream stack
identical and avoids a separate auxiliary head.

\paragraph{Multi-signal similarity scoring.}
The text retriever $r$ combines evidence channels of decreasing
precision: a high-precision channel (exact substring or
exact-word-overlap), a medium-precision channel
(pronunciation-level or longest-common-subsequence match), and a
low-precision channel (character-set overlap or bigram Jaccard).
The combined score takes the schematic form
\begin{equation}
  s(h, \tilde{y}) = \sum_{c\,\in\,\text{channels}} w_c\,
    \mathrm{sim}_c(h, \tilde{y}) - \mathrm{pen}(h),\quad
  \mathcal{H}^\prime = \mathrm{TopK}\{h : s(h, \tilde{y}) > \tau\},
  \label{eq:retrieve-score}
\end{equation}
with the weights $\{w_c\}$ and threshold $\tau$ chosen so that any
candidate supported only by low-precision channels falls below
$\tau$ while a single high-precision channel suffices to clear it.
A short-word penalty $\mathrm{pen}(h)$ suppresses candidates of
length $\le 5$ characters whose modular matching is statistically
dominated by accidental substring containment. We use two routes
specialised by script: a CJK route with a pronunciation channel
built on tone-stripped pinyin (the dominant Mandarin error mode is
same-pronunciation character substitution, against which a pure
character-overlap score cannot discriminate), and an alphabetic
route with a content-word overlap channel and a longest-common-
subsequence channel. The CJK / alphabetic split is enforced by a
fixed CJK character ratio. Specific weights, thresholds and
short-word penalty values are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:retrieve-constants}.

\paragraph{Global discriminative-gap check.}
For the alphabetic route we additionally enforce a
discriminative-gap constraint: if the score ratio between the
first- and tenth-ranked candidates falls below a fixed factor, the
operator returns the empty set and the fine decoder $f$ runs in
no-hotword mode. The constraint encodes the intuition that when no
candidate is clearly preferred, every $K$-element output is
essentially noise; injecting noise into the prompt is worse than
dropping the hotword line entirely, because the LLM responds to
nonsensical hotwords by emitting partial copies of them. The CJK
route does not need this check because the pinyin channel
produces a natural scale gap between matches and non-matches at the
syllable boundary; the alphabetic content-word and substring
channels produce smaller between-candidate gaps and require the
explicit check.

\paragraph{Training--inference alignment.}
The retriever is the inference-time half of an SFT augmentation
pipeline. To match the training and inference candidate
distributions, each training prompt is built from the
\textbf{real terms} (the ground-truth hotword list), \textbf{hard
negatives} computed by the same character / pinyin-overlap logic
as above against the ground-truth transcript (and not against the
coarse decode $\tilde{y}$, which is unavailable at SFT time), and
\textbf{random distractors} from the global pool; see
Section~\ref{subsec:hotword-pool} for the exposure schedule. Three
augmentation hooks line up with three failure modes of the
inference retriever: independent ground-truth drop with
probability $p_\text{miss}$ mirrors retriever recall miss; full
hotword-line drop with probability $p_\text{drop}$ mirrors the
discriminative-gap fallback; and a uniformly sampled $K$ between
$1$ and $K_\text{max}$ mirrors retriever-output-size variance
across pools. GRPO (Section~\ref{subsec:grpo}) then pushes the
binary $c \in \hat{y}$ / $c \notin \hat{y}$ decision at the margin
through the hotword match-accuracy reward.

\paragraph{Relation to prior retrieval-based contextual ASR.}
Three concurrent lines target the same retrieve-then-bias
structure. Lakomkin et al.~\cite{lakomkin2025contextual} use a
joint-embedding CLAP-style retriever and align it with GRPO. Sun
et al.~\cite{sun2025br} (BR-ASR) train a retrieval head on top of
hotword embeddings for very large pools (200\,k entries).
Yang et al.~\cite{yang2024ctc} use a CTC auxiliary decoder as the
coarse decode. The closest neighbour from the speech-RAG line is
Min et al.~\cite{min2024speechrag}, who skip $g$ entirely with a
learned speech adapter that maps audio directly into a frozen text
retriever, and the speaker-querying line in TS-ASR
(Section~\ref{sec:tsasr}). The differentiating choice of our
operator is that $r$ is parameter-free and routed by script: it
has a small number of global constants (four channel weights, one
acceptance threshold, one discriminative ratio, two augmentation
drop probabilities), each constrained structurally rather than
learned. This trades data-driven gain for cross-domain
transferability; new domains require updating the pool but not
retraining the retriever.

\paragraph{Baseline contrast.}
Table~\ref{tab:hotwords_baseline} compares AmphionASR with
Fun-ASR-Nano-2512~\cite{funasrnano} in both protocols. The oracle
prompt protocol shows that the baseline gains $2$--$3$ WER points
from hotwords on both languages, but the gain is consistently
smaller than the AmphionASR delta and reverses past $k = 10$ on
Mandarin (degrading from $4.55$ at $k=5$ to $5.53$ at $k=20$). In
the retrieve-client protocol the baseline's Recall@$K$ does not
exceed $0.27\%$ in any configuration; the corresponding downstream
WER remains close to the no-hotword baseline. We report the
contrast without attempting to attribute the Recall@$K$ result.

\subsection{Analysis}

\paragraph{Oracle prompt: large gain from a small list with a mild
non-monotonic tail.}
Five hotwords reduce Mandarin CER by $3.34$ absolute points (from
$6.01$\,\% at $k=0$ to $2.67$\,\% at $k=5$) and English WER by
$2.99$ absolute points (from $7.68$\,\% at $k=0$ to $4.69$\,\% at
$k=10$, the English minimum). Going from five to twenty hotwords
has a small but consistent negative effect (roughly $+0.10$ CER
on Mandarin and $+0.10$ WER on English from the respective
minima). The oracle protocol pads with random distractors rather
than the hard negatives used during Stage~3 training; the
underlying cause of the slight upturn has not yet been
investigated.

\paragraph{Retrieve client: high recall, language-asymmetric biased-WER.}
On Mandarin the retriever attains Recall@$5$ of $91\%$ and
Recall@$K$ above $94\%$ from $K=10$ onwards, which translates into
B-WER under $4\%$ and total WER under $3.6\%$ -- about $0.9$ CER
points above the corresponding $k=5$ oracle row. On English the
retriever plateaus at Recall@$K \approx 78\%$ for $K=20$, with
B-WER bounded at $19.28\%$ and total WER at $7.34\%$. The
Mandarin / English asymmetry reflects the difference between the
pinyin and content-word matching channels at the same pool size:
the Mandarin pool can disambiguate same-pronunciation candidates
through the pinyin channel, whereas the English alphabetic route
relies on substring matching and is more sensitive to coarse-decode
errors in $\tilde{y}$.

\paragraph{Retrieve-vs-oracle gap.}
The retrieve client trails the oracle client by roughly $0.9$ CER
points on Mandarin and $2.6$ WER points on English at $K=5$. The
gap has two contributing components -- retrieval recall loss
(non-zero everywhere) and dilution by retrieval false positives
(more prominent on the alphabetic route, where the
discriminative-gap check leaves more borderline candidates on the
prompt) -- which we do not attempt to decompose further in this
report.

\paragraph{Baseline contrast.}
Fun-ASR-Nano-2512 benefits from the oracle prompt at a smaller
absolute scale than AmphionASR ($-2.50$\,CER on CV17~zh from $k=0$
to $k=5$ vs.\ $-3.34$ for AmphionASR) and reverses past $k=10$.
In the retrieve protocol the baseline's Recall@$K$ does not exceed
$0.27\%$ in any configuration, so the comparison degenerates to
no-hotword ASR. We note the contrast but do not attempt to
attribute it; the retriever in
Section~\ref{subsec:retrieve-algorithm} is paired with SFT
augmentations that the baseline was not trained for, and a direct
attribution would require running both systems through a common
retriever and augmentation pipeline.
