\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% --- Background: ASR landscape ---
End-to-end speech recognition has matured from hybrid HMM-DNN systems
\citep{hinton2012deep} to large-scale weakly-supervised models such as
Whisper \citep{radford2023whisper}, which demonstrate that scaling data
and model size delivers strong recognition across languages and acoustic
conditions. In parallel, large language models (LLMs) have become the
dominant backbone for text understanding and generation, and several
recent systems -- Qwen-Audio \citep{chu2023qwenaudio},
SALMONN \citep{tang2024salmonn}, Step-Audio~2 \citep{stepaudio22025},
Kimi-Audio \citep{kimiaudio2025} and Qwen3-ASR \citep{qwen2025qwen3asr}
-- show that connecting a strong audio encoder to a pre-trained LLM
yields a single model that transcribes, understands and follows
instructions in one pass.

% --- Motivation: real-world capabilities the LLM brings ---
The LLM backbone is more than a stronger language model: its prompt
interface is a natural place to express the kind of side information
that real deployments require. Two pieces of side information are
particularly common and particularly awkward for end-to-end ASR:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hotwords.} Names, jargon, product SKUs, and rare
        entities that a generic ASR model is unlikely to have memorised.
        Classical solutions involve external biasing graphs or
        on-the-fly LM rescoring; an LLM can simply accept the list as
        text in its prompt.
  \item \textbf{Target speaker.} In multi-talker audio the user often
        wants the transcript of \emph{one} known speaker. Source
        separation followed by ASR is brittle; an end-to-end LLM that
        receives a short reference utterance can be trained to decode
        only the matching speaker.
\end{itemize}

% --- This work ---
We describe \textbf{AmphionASR}, a 4.7\,B-parameter SpeechLLM
($4.3$\,B distinct after \texttt{lm\_head} tying) that treats both
capabilities as first-class citizens of the prompt, while remaining
competitive on standard Mandarin and English ASR benchmarks.

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A 4.7\,B Mandarin--English SpeechLLM recipe.} We pair
        the Qwen3-AuT audio tower (Whisper-style 24-layer Transformer
        at $d{=}1024$, 314\,M parameters) with the
        Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 \citep{qwen2025qwen3} LLM through a
        9.2\,M-parameter Linear--SwooshR--Linear projector
        \citep{yao2024zipformer} and four learnable modality-boundary
        prompt vectors. On a curated public-plus-internal corpus the
        model reaches $1.8$\% CER on AISHELL-1, $7.17$\% WER on
        LibriSpeech \texttt{test-clean} and $14.99$\% MER on the TALCS
        Mandarin--English code-switching test set \citep{li2022talcs}
        (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
  \item \textbf{Strong hotword conditioning out of the prompt.} With
        five in-prompt hotwords the CommonVoice CER drops from $6.57$\%
        to $2.83$\% (Mandarin) and the WER from $9.65$\% to $4.89$\%
        (English); we further show diminishing returns beyond five
        hotwords (Section~\ref{sec:hotwords}).
  \item \textbf{Target-speaker recognition with reference audio.} A
        short reference utterance suffices to make the model attend to a
        single speaker in mixed audio. On our internal hotword + target-speaker
        test we reach $14.29$\% MER; we report and analyse the
        substantially weaker numbers on the public LibriMix benchmarks
        (Section~\ref{sec:tsasr}).
  \item \textbf{An honest accounting of gaps.} Explicit
        noise-robustness training is not yet integrated; we describe the
        target benchmark and planned recipe so that the next release can
        be measured against this report
        (Section~\ref{sec:noise}).
\end{itemize}
